        <h2>Wing Coloration in <em>Colias</em> Butterflies</h2>
        <h3 style="font-size:var(--text-lg);color:var(--color-text-muted);font-weight:400;font-style:italic;margin-bottom:1.5rem;">A legacy of climate change measured in wing color</h3>
        <p>Two ongoing TrEnCh projects in the mountains of Colorado are repeating historical field observations and field and lab experiments—as well as measuring historical specimens—to examine ecological and evolutionary responses to recent climate change.</p>
        <p>In the late 1950s, Charles Remington, a Yale entomology professor, studied <em>Colias</em> butterflies at elevations in Colorado to explore the ecology of high elevation insects. He was measuring melanin pigmentation in butterfly wing color and genetics in butterflies at different sites going up in elevation. The collection he left behind, now at the Rocky Mountain Biological Laboratory (RMBL) in Gothic, Colorado, has become an invaluable resource: a living museum of how these butterflies—and the climate around them—have changed.</p>

    <p class="section-label">Butterfly Biology</p>
    <h2>The challenges of being a thermal specialist</h2>
    <p style="color:var(--color-text-muted);max-width:62ch;margin-bottom:var(--space-8);">Butterflies are highly susceptible to temperature fluctuations due to their small size and ectothermy. Their ability to fly is essential for survival and reproduction, but flight is only possible within a narrow range of body temperatures. Thus, climate change may affect how often butterflies have opportunities for reproduction and increase their risk of overheating, thereby threatening their condition and survival.</p>
    <div class="findings-grid">
      <div class="finding-card">
        <h3>Evolutionary plasticity in wing coloration</h3>
        <p>Our field work documents that populations and species have shifted their phenologies and negative abundances over 60 years of climate change.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="finding-card">
        <h3>Plasticity in wing color can facilitate acclimation</h3>
        <p>Our development work shows that butterflies can adjust their wing color in response to temperature during development, with important consequences for thermal performance.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="finding-card">
        <h3>Interactions among plasticity shifts in wing color</h3>
        <p>We use our models to ask whether plasticity in wing coloration mediates interactions among plasticity shifts in phenology, behavior, and thermal physiology.</p>
      </div>
    </div>
    <div class="callout" style="margin-top:2rem;">
      <p>We began with the simple expectation that butterflies would evolve lighter wings at higher elevations where it gets colder. The answer isn't what you might expect.</p>
    </div>

        <h2>The mystery of dusty museum boxes</h2>
        <p>Entomologist César Nufio, now at the University of Colorado, grew up in Boulder. While visiting the University of Colorado Museum of Natural History collection of grasshoppers, gathering dust atop a wooden bookcase in the attic since the 1960s, he discovered a systematic collection that would become central to TrEnCh research.</p>
        <p>The collection was created by Gordon Alexander, who made it his life's work. Nufio has worked to contact an overdue assessment of population changes.</p>

<section class="section section-alt">
  <div class="container">
    <p class="section-label">Key Figures &amp; Findings</p>
    <h2 class="section-title">What we've learned from grasshoppers</h2>
    <div class="findings-grid">
      <div class="finding-card">
        <h3>Thermal tolerances and preferences vary along the elevation gradient</h3>
        <p>We have documented that thermal preferences—measured as temperatures animals voluntarily select in a gradient arena—vary along the elevation series along the mountain, reflecting local adaptation.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="finding-card">
        <h3>High- and low-elevation populations differ in their developmental rate</h3>
        <p>Populations from contrasting elevations have important developmental differences: high-elevation individuals develop faster to compensate for shorter seasons. This may shift as climate warms.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="finding-card">
        <h3>Growing interest in physiological and genetic mechanisms</h3>
        <p>This has led to new projects investigating the physiological and genetic mechanisms underlying the phenological and demographic responses observed in the field. A new NSF Rules of Life grant supports this work.</p>
      </div>
